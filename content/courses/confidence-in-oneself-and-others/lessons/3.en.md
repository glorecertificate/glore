questions:
  - question: You are asked to speak for two minutes about your volunteering at a school assembly. You have never done this and feel nervous. Which response shows healthy self-belief?
    options:
      - text: Say yes, remind yourself of other times you spoke in front of people, and prepare a short outline with a friend listening.
        correct: true
      - text: Say no, because you are sure to freeze.
      - text: Say yes, but do not prepare, since being natural is better.
      - text: Ask someone else to speak first so you can copy what they do.
    explanation: Confidence rests on past evidence and preparation. Using what you already did and asking for a rehearsal makes the task feel manageable. Refusing avoids the chance to grow, skipping preparation relies on luck, and copying another person leaves your own message out.
  - question: A new volunteer at a youth centre makes a mistake and posts the wrong date in the group chat. How can the team build trust with her?
    options:
      - text: Tell her she should not post in the chat again.
      - text: Correct the date quietly yourself and say nothing.
      - text: Ask her to explain to the whole team what went wrong.
      - text: Correct the date together with her, say that mistakes like this happen, and let her keep posting.
        correct: true
    explanation: Fixing it together shows that the team expects her to learn and carry on, so her confidence and the team's trust in her both grow. Taking the task away tells her she cannot be relied on, fixing it silently means she never finds out, and making her explain herself to everyone can embarrass her.
  - question: You promised to bring the sound equipment for Sunday's neighbourhood event, but on Saturday you realise you cannot get it. What builds the most trust?
    options:
      - text: Hope someone else will bring theirs and say nothing.
      - text: Tell the organiser today, explain what happened, and offer a way to solve it, such as asking a friend who owns a speaker.
        correct: true
      - text: Bring a smaller speaker on Sunday and say it is the best you could do.
      - text: Wait until the event starts and then apologise.
    explanation: Telling the organiser early gives them time to find another solution, and offering an alternative shows you still take responsibility. Staying silent or apologising at the last minute leaves them without options, and surprising them with a lesser substitute hides the problem.
  - question: You are coordinating a charity bake sale. A shy teenager offers to handle the cash box. How do you show trust in her?
    options:
      - text: Say yes but stand over her and count each coin.
      - text: Say yes, show her how the box works, and check in at the end of the day without hovering.
        correct: true
      - text: Say no, because it is too much responsibility for someone her age.
      - text: Say yes, then leave without explaining anything.
    explanation: Giving a clear introduction and then room to work shows real trust and gives her a chance to grow into it. Watching every move signals you do not trust her, refusing reflects only age, and leaving her without any guidance sets her up to fail.
  - question: A teammate at a summer job says, "I am just not a numbers person" when asked to help with the weekly report. What is the most supportive response?
    options:
      - text: Agree, and take the report yourself.
      - text: Tell them they are being silly and should just do it.
      - text: Ask which part worries them, offer to go through the first report together, and point to a similar task they handled well.
        correct: true
    explanation: Asking what worries them takes the fear seriously, working through the first report together gives a safe start, and pointing to past successes builds their belief. Agreeing keeps them stuck in that view, and brushing it off dismisses a real feeling.

Read these situations and think about how you would respond.

At an elderly care home, a resident who used to be a teacher offers to help run a reading hour, but the staff hesitate because she sometimes forgets names.

A volunteer in an online campaign is asked to represent the team in a video call with a partner organisation. She says she is not good enough.

In a student flat, a housemate returns money he borrowed two weeks late and without saying anything.
