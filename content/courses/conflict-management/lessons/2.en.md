---
title: What conflict management means
---

This is the GloRe definition:

> Ability to cope with emotions that emerge in dealing with diverging opinions and interests, and the ability to reach an outcome beneficial to all parties, over one or more issues, through dialogue.

Disagreement is normal. People with different needs and ideas disagree, and a team that never disagrees may be keeping things quiet. What matters is how you handle it.

## Coping with your emotions

In a disagreement you may feel angry, anxious or hurt. These feelings are normal and they give you information. They also make it harder to listen, so the first task is to notice them. A pause, a few slow breaths, or a short break is often enough to bring your tone down. You can say, "I need a few minutes, and then I want to continue."

At university, you read a message from a teammate saying that you left your part unfinished. You feel your face getting hot. Instead of answering at once, you wait an hour, reread the message and realise the deadline was unclear.

## Understanding opinions and interests

What people ask for is their position: "We must sort by size." Why they ask is their interest: "Parents should find clothes quickly." Two positions can clash while the interests fit together. Asking "What is most important to you about this?" turns an argument into a shared problem.

Two flatmates argue about noise. One studies at night, and the other starts work at six. The positions are "Stop playing music" and "Stop complaining". The interests are quiet for studying and sleep before an early shift.

## Reaching an outcome beneficial to all

The goal is a solution that meets the main need of each person, without one side winning or both giving up everything. Come up with several options before you choose one, and check whether everyone can live with it. For the flatmates, an agreement could be quiet hours from ten at night, with headphones for music and a short note when a late night is exceptional.

## Dialogue, not blame

Speak about what happened and how it affects you, not about what the other person is like. "When the schedule changed without a message, I felt left out" is easier to hear than "You never include me." Listen to the answer and check that you understood it before you reply.

## When to ask for help

Some situations are not for you to solve alone. If someone harasses or threatens you, makes comments about your body, origin or beliefs, or if you feel unsafe, do not try to resolve it with that person. Speak to the coordinator or the person responsible for safeguarding in your organisation. A mediator can also help when two people cannot talk any more.
