---
title: What creativity and innovation mean
---

This is the GloRe definition:

> Ability to generate new or alternative solutions to problems, to think outside the box, and to find solutions to improve current practices.

Creativity is a habit you can build: asking "what else could we do?" before settling on the first answer. Innovation happens when you test one of those answers and it makes things work better.

## Generating new or alternative solutions

The first idea is rarely the only one, and it is often just the most familiar. Generating alternatives means giving yourself time to list several options, including ones that sound a bit strange, and judging them afterwards. If you judge each idea the moment it appears, most of them never get written down.

At a youth centre, the team wants more teenagers to join the Thursday workshop. The first idea is to print more posters. Written down next to it: ask last year's participants to bring a friend, run a short session in the school canteen, let teenagers choose the topic. The posters may still be part of the plan, but now there is a choice.

## Thinking outside the box

This means finding the assumption that limits you and asking what happens if you drop it. Most "boxes" are rules nobody remembers choosing: the meeting is always at six, the leaflet is always on paper, newcomers always start with the dull jobs.

For a university project on food waste, a student group assumes that they need a survey. A teammate asks what else would show them how much food is thrown away. They end up weighing the leftovers from one canteen for a week, and the numbers say more than any questionnaire.

## Improving current practices

Innovation can be a small change to how something is done now: a shorter form, a clearer welcome for new volunteers, a different order for the tasks. Start by noticing what frustrates people or wastes time, because that is where improvements are usually hiding.

Then test the change in a small way first. Try the new form with one group. Run the new schedule for two weeks. Ask the people affected what they think, and keep, adjust or drop it based on what you learn. An idea that fails in a small test costs little, and you learn something from it.

At work, a new colleague sees that customers keep asking the same three questions by email. She suggests a short answer sheet for the team. The first version has gaps, the team fills them in, and email volume drops.

## Ideas belong to the group

Creative ideas often start rough, and they improve when other people add to them. Saying "yes, and what if..." keeps an idea alive long enough to see what it could become. Giving credit to the people who helped shape it makes them more willing to share the next one.
