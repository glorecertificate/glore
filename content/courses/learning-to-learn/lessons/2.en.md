---
title: What learning to learn means
---

This is the GloRe definition:

> Ability to understand one's own learning style and aims, and to implement a personal learning plan accordingly.

Learning to learn means taking charge of your own learning. You work out how you take things in, you decide what you want to learn and why, and you build a plan that fits you. School and courses teach content, but you will keep learning for the rest of your life, often with nobody setting the tasks.

## Understanding your own learning style

People take in information in different ways. Some remember best what they see in a diagram. Others need to talk it through, or write it out, or try it with their hands. Most of us mix several, and the best mix depends on the topic and on the day. You find yours by noticing what has worked in the past and by experimenting.

At a youth exchange, you have to learn the basics of first aid in one weekend. You notice you retain more when you practise on a partner than when you listen to the trainer. Next time, you will ask for more hands-on practice, or find a way to create it.

Your learning style can change over time. Pay attention to the conditions that help too: quiet or company, morning or evening, short bursts or long stretches.

## Understanding your learning aims

A clear aim keeps you going when the work gets slow. "Get better at Spanish" is vague. "Hold a ten-minute conversation with my host family before I leave in June" tells you what to practise and when you have succeeded. It also helps to know why you want it, because the reason is what you hold on to when motivation drops.

A student who volunteers at a youth centre wants to run activities in English. She replaces "improve my English" with "lead one 30-minute game in English by the end of the term." Now she knows which words to practise.

## Implementing a personal learning plan

A plan turns an aim into steps. It says what you will do, how often, with whom or with what, and how you will know you are progressing. A good plan is small enough to start this week: one resource, one practice slot in your calendar, one person to ask for feedback.

At work, a new team member has to learn a spreadsheet tool within a month. She lists three skills she needs, finds a short course for the first, and asks a colleague to review her work every Friday. After two weeks, she sees that the course is too basic and swaps it for a harder one.

Plans need adjusting. When something does not work, change the method, the time or the resource before you give up on the aim. Reviewing your plan every couple of weeks, and asking someone else what they see, helps you spot what to change.

## Learning with others

You do not have to learn alone. Asking someone more experienced for advice, joining a study group, or exchanging skills with another volunteer are all part of a good plan, and they are often faster than doing it by yourself.
