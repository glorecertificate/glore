---
title: What networking and public relations mean
---

Here is the GloRe definition:

> Ability to create connections with people or institutions external to the organization and keep them active in order to create productive work relationships.

There are three ideas in it: creating connections, keeping them active and making them productive.

## Creating connections

Connections begin with a simple step: you introduce yourself and say what you do in one or two sentences. "I volunteer at a youth centre where we help teenagers with homework" is enough. Then you ask something about the other person and listen. Most people like to talk about their work when someone shows real interest.

The connections can be with people, such as a teacher, a shop owner or a journalist, or with institutions, such as a library, a council, a school or a company. Public relations is the part of this skill that deals with how your organisation is seen from outside. It means being honest, polite and reliable whenever you speak for the group.

At a volunteer fair, you talk to someone from another association. You find out that both groups are organising a clean-up in the same month. You decide to share the cost of the bags.

## Keeping them active

A contact you never speak to again fades quickly. You keep it alive with small, regular actions: a thank-you message after a meeting, an invitation to your next event, a link to an article that may interest them, or a short update about what happened thanks to their help.

A good rhythm is simple. After a first meeting, write within a few days. After that, a message every few months is enough. You write to say hello, not only when you need something.

At university, you meet a professor after a talk and ask a question. You send a short email to thank them, and months later you tell them how their answer helped your project.

## Making relationships productive

Productive does not mean selfish. It means both sides gain something. Before you ask for help, think about what you can offer: time, volunteers, visibility, information or a thank-you. Be clear about what you want, and accept a no politely, because the door can reopen later.

At work, a colleague from another department introduces you to a supplier. You return the favour by telling her about a training course. Connections like these become useful because people trust each other, and trust grows when you do what you said you would do.
