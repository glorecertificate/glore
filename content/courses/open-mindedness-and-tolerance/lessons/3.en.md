title: Practice
questions:
  - question: During a group dinner, a fellow volunteer explains a religious practice you do not know. What is a respectful response?
    options:
      - text: Say that it sounds strange and ask why they do it that way.
      - text: Ask them to tell you more about what it means for them and what would help, and listen to the answer.
        correct: true
      - text: Nod and say nothing, then look it up and decide privately what you think.
    explanation: Asking with real interest shows respect and gives you the information you need to plan around it. Calling something strange puts the person on the defensive. Silence misses the chance to understand and leaves the person without the support they might need.
  - question: Local volunteers organise an event differently from how you are used to. You think your way is more efficient. What do you do?
    options:
      - text: Try their way for this event, observe what works, and share your feedback afterwards.
        correct: true
      - text: Insist on your way because you know it works.
      - text: Agree openly and then do it your way when nobody is looking.
    explanation: Trying another way gives you real evidence. Their method may fit the local conditions better than you expect, and your feedback will be taken more seriously afterwards. Insisting or pretending to agree damages trust.
  - question: In a class debate, a classmate defends an opinion you strongly disagree with. What shows open-mindedness?
    options:
      - text: Interrupt to correct what she is saying.
      - text: Roll your eyes and stop listening.
      - text: Stay silent now and avoid her later.
      - text: Listen to her reasons, ask a question to understand them, and then explain your own view calmly.
        correct: true
    explanation: You can disagree and still be open. Listening to the reasons first lets you respond to what she actually says, and she is more likely to listen to you in return. The other options end the exchange without learning anything.
  - question: A teammate keeps making jokes that put down people of a certain background. Others laugh, and he says everyone is entitled to an opinion. What is the best response?
    options:
      - text: Accept it, because tolerance means letting everyone speak.
      - text: Laugh along to avoid an awkward moment.
      - text: Say clearly that these jokes are not acceptable, and tell the coordinator if they continue.
        correct: true
    explanation: Tolerance protects people's right to hold different views, not behaviour that demeans others. Saying so calmly sets the limit, and the coordinator is the right person if it continues. Laughing or staying silent tells others that it is fine.
  - question: A new colleague comes from a country about which you have heard many generalisations. How do you approach her?
    options:
      - text: Assume the generalisations are true for her.
      - text: Never mention where she is from, to be safe.
      - text: Ask her to explain what everyone in her country thinks.
      - text: Get to know her as an individual, and when an assumption comes to mind, check it against what she actually says and does.
        correct: true
    explanation: People are individuals, and a generalisation tells you little about the person in front of you. Noticing and testing your assumptions is the core of open-mindedness. Avoiding the topic or asking her to speak for a whole country treats her as a label.
---

Read these situations and think about how you would respond.

At a language cafe, an older participant says he does not trust online banking and prefers to do everything at the counter.

In a flat share, your housemate keeps a quiet routine of prayer in the morning that you find a little surprising.

In a team chat, a colleague shares a political opinion that is very different from yours, with no insults.
