questions:
  - question: Your youth centre holds a fundraiser on Saturday. The posters must be printed, the snacks bought, the sign repainted and the playlist chosen. Which task do you start today?
    options:
      - text: The playlist, because it is quick and fun.
      - text: Sending the posters to print, because they have the earliest deadline and the publicity depends on them.
        correct: true
      - text: Repainting the sign, because it takes the longest.
      - text: The snacks, because food is what visitors remember.
    explanation: Prioritising means looking at deadlines and dependencies. Nobody can come to the fundraiser if they never see the poster, and printing takes time. The sign and the snacks matter, but they can be done later, and the playlist can wait until last.
  - question: At a festival, one volunteer always gets the hardest shifts because she never says no. As coordinator, what is the best way to share the work?
    options:
      - text: Keep the same plan, because she has not complained.
      - text: Give her fewer shifts without telling anyone.
      - text: Ask the others to volunteer, and hope it balances out.
      - text: Ask everyone about their availability, then rotate the hard shifts openly.
        correct: true
    explanation: Equitable sharing starts from knowing what people can give. Asking everyone and rotating the demanding shifts spreads the load in a way people can see. Silence from one person is not agreement, and hoping for balance leaves the same person carrying the weight.
  - question: You have an assignment due in three weeks and you have not started. What plan gives you the best chance of finishing on time?
    options:
      - text: Split it into steps with a date for each, and leave a few days free at the end.
        correct: true
      - text: Wait for a motivated week, then do it all at once.
      - text: Start with the introduction and write until it is done.
      - text: Do the easiest part first and decide the rest later.
    explanation: Steps with dates turn a large task into small ones and show early if you are late. The spare days absorb delays. Waiting or improvising leaves no way to notice a problem until it is too late.
  - question: You planned a river clean-up for forty people, but only eight volunteers and a small budget are confirmed. What do you do?
    options:
      - text: Keep the plan and hope more people come.
      - text: Cancel the event, because it cannot be done as planned.
      - text: Reduce the scope to a shorter stretch, borrow the tools and tell the volunteers the new plan.
        correct: true
      - text: Ask each volunteer to work twice as hard.
    explanation: Plans should match the resources you really have. A smaller scope still reaches the goal in part, and it is honest with the team. Hoping and asking people to overwork both ignore the limits, and cancelling gives up more than is necessary.
  - question: Your coordinator moves a deadline two days earlier. Which response shows good organization?
    options:
      - text: Keep working as before and see what happens.
      - text: Check what is already done, reorder the remaining tasks, and tell the team what changes.
        correct: true
      - text: Tell the coordinator that the new date is impossible.
    explanation: A change of deadline calls for a new look at the plan. Checking progress and reordering tasks shows what is possible, and telling the team keeps everyone working on the same version. Refusing without checking, or ignoring the change, solves nothing.

Read these situations and think about how you would organize the work.

At an elderly care home, you and three other volunteers must prepare a Sunday afternoon party. You have a small budget, and one of you can only come on Saturday.

In a student association, the person who runs the website is leaving in two weeks. Nobody has written down how the site is updated.

At a sports club, the tournament registration closes on Friday. You have a list of fifty tasks and four evenings left.
