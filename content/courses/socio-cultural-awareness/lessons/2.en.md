---
title: What socio-cultural and multicultural awareness means
---

This is the GloRe definition:

> Ability to cope with social and cultural differences and specificities; ability to make others part of a group, taking their differences (rights, needs and opinions) into consideration.

Every person grows up with habits, values and expectations shaped by family, community, religion, language, income, gender, age and many other things. Awareness starts with seeing that your way is one among many, and that other ways are equally real to the people who live them.

## Coping with social and cultural differences

Differences show up in small things: how close people stand, whether it is polite to look someone in the eye, how direct a "no" can be, what time "on time" means, who speaks first in a meeting. None of these are right or wrong. They are different habits, and they only cause friction when each side assumes theirs is the only one.

Coping with differences means staying curious when you feel puzzled or uncomfortable. You pause before you judge, you ask, and you adjust. It also means not turning one person into a spokesperson for a whole country or religion. People differ within every culture, so a stereotype is a poor guide to the person in front of you.

At an international youth exchange, a volunteer from Finland is annoyed that her Portuguese roommate talks late into the evening. She asks him about it, and he explains that in his family dinner and conversation are the best part of the day. They agree on quiet hours on weeknights and long talks at the weekend.

## Making others part of the group

Including people means checking whether they can take part fully: Is the time of the meeting good for them? Is the language clear? Is the activity comfortable for them? Is there a role in which they can contribute?

At a university, a student notices that the debating society always meets at a bar. Some members do not drink for religious or health reasons, and some cannot afford it. She suggests alternating with a café and a park, and attendance goes up.

Think of who is on the margins of your group: someone new, someone who speaks little of the common language, someone who is older or younger than the others. A small gesture helps, such as sitting with them, learning to say their name correctly, or inviting their view directly.

## Taking differences into consideration

The definition names three things to consider: rights, needs and opinions. Rights are what every person is entitled to, such as being treated with dignity and without discrimination. Needs are practical, such as a prayer space, step-free access, halal or vegetarian food, or time to take a call with family abroad. Opinions are what people think, and they deserve to be heard even when you disagree.

Respecting a difference does not mean agreeing with everything. If a practice harms someone or denies their rights, you speak up. What you avoid is dismissing a person because their habits are unfamiliar to you.

At work, a manager plans a team lunch on a day that overlaps with a religious festival for one of her colleagues. When he mentions it, she moves the lunch and thanks him for saying so. A week later, he suggests a way to run the team's meetings in two languages.
