title: Practice
questions:
  - question: Your sports club has a new teammate from Eritrea who rarely speaks at team meetings. You are tempted to decide that she is shy. What is the best approach?
    options:
      - text: Assume she is shy and avoid putting pressure on her.
      - text: Talk with her privately, ask how she prefers to take part, and share what the team needs to make it easier.
        correct: true
      - text: Tell her that here everyone is expected to speak up.
    explanation: Asking avoids a guess based on a stereotype and shows interest in her. People can be quiet for many reasons, including language, habit or fear of making mistakes. Assuming shyness or demanding speech both leave out her point of view.
  - question: You organise a volunteer weekend and plan a barbecue for Friday evening. One volunteer mentions that his Shabbat starts at sunset on Fridays, so he does not travel or join events after that. What do you do?
    options:
      - text: Tell him it is just one evening and he can come anyway.
      - text: Keep the barbecue and send him a plate afterwards.
      - text: Thank him for telling you and move the barbecue to a time that works for everyone, or ask the group for options.
        correct: true
      - text: Cancel the barbecue entirely.
    explanation: Adapting the plan takes his need into account and keeps the group together, and asking the group shares the decision. Dismissing his need or sending a plate afterwards leaves him out, and cancelling the whole event would be more than the situation needs.
  - question: In a mixed team at a refugee support centre, a volunteer from Italy says that people from a certain country are "always late". Which response is most helpful?
    options:
      - text: Say that people differ, and that being late might be linked to buses, work shifts or paperwork, so you could ask the people involved.
        correct: true
      - text: Laugh, because it is a harmless joke.
      - text: Tell the volunteer that they are racist.
      - text: Say nothing, but start avoiding the volunteer.
    explanation: Questioning the generalisation without attacking the person opens a conversation, and offering other explanations shows why stereotypes miss the cause. Laughing supports the stereotype, an accusation makes the volunteer defensive, and silence changes nothing.
  - question: A youth centre plans a poster for an event. The team wants the images to feel welcoming to everyone in the neighbourhood. What should they do?
    options:
      - text: Use images of only one community, since they are the largest group.
      - text: Use stock photos of smiling people from many countries and nothing more.
      - text: Choose images by what looks nice and leave the rest to chance.
      - text: Ask local residents from different backgrounds what they would like to see, and include their ideas.
        correct: true
    explanation: Involving the people the poster speaks to makes them part of the choice and brings in views the team may not have. Showing one group leaves others out, generic photos can look tokenistic, and chance does not reflect real people.
  - question: At a language café, a retired man keeps correcting the pronunciation of a young participant in front of the group. She looks uncomfortable. What do you do?
    options:
      - text: Let it continue, since correction helps learning.
      - text: Ask him to leave.
      - text: Thank him for helping, suggest saving corrections for a private chat, and ask her how she likes to be corrected.
        correct: true
      - text: Correct him in front of the group in the same way.
    explanation: People have different views on correction, and what helps one person can embarrass another. Redirecting kindly respects both of them and asks the learner what she wants. Letting it go ignores her discomfort, and asking him to leave or humiliating him treats him unfairly.
---

Read these situations and think about how you would respond.

At a festival, the volunteer team includes people who fast during Ramadan, and the daily schedule has lunch break at noon for everyone.

At a care home, a resident from Poland would like to talk about his country, but the other residents have not shown interest.

In a student flat, you share a kitchen with people from four countries, and the cleaning rota keeps causing arguments about what counts as clean.
