questions:
  - question: Before a busy festival shift, you feel a tight chest and your thoughts are racing. What is the best first step?
    options:
      - text: Ignore it and hurry to the stand.
      - text: Drink a strong coffee to wake up.
      - text: Take a minute to breathe slowly, name what you feel, and decide what you will do first.
        correct: true
      - text: Cancel your shift without telling anyone.
    explanation: A short pause with slow breathing calms the body, and naming the feeling helps you think. Deciding on the first small step gives the mind something to hold on to. Ignoring the signs makes them grow, and leaving without a word creates a new problem for your team.
  - question: A visitor shouts at you about a mistake that was not yours. What do you do?
    options:
      - text: Shout back, so the visitor knows you are not weak.
      - text: Keep your voice calm, let the visitor finish, say you can see they are upset and explain what you can do.
        correct: true
      - text: Walk away without saying anything.
      - text: Blame the colleague who made the mistake.
    explanation: A calm voice and a short acknowledgement often lower the tension for both sides. Offering a next step moves the conversation to solutions. Shouting adds fuel, and leaving or blaming someone does not solve the problem. If the person stays aggressive, call a colleague or the coordinator.
  - question: It is exam week. You work until 3 a.m. every night and barely eat. Which change helps most?
    options:
      - text: Sleep less and study more, since the exam is soon.
      - text: Skip meals, to save time.
      - text: Wait until the exam is over to rest.
      - text: Plan study blocks with breaks, regular meals and sleep, and talk to a tutor if the workload is too heavy.
        correct: true
    explanation: Rest and food help memory and concentration, so a plan with breaks usually gives better results than extra hours. Telling a tutor about an unrealistic workload is a sensible step. Cutting sleep and meals makes stress worse the next day.
  - question: After a hard shift at a refugee support centre, you feel heavy because of a story you heard. What should you do?
    options:
      - text: Tell your coordinator or someone you trust how you feel, and give yourself time to rest.
        correct: true
      - text: Hide it, because volunteers should be strong.
      - text: Take on more shifts to keep busy.
      - text: Stop thinking about it, as it is not your problem.
    explanation: Hearing difficult stories can weigh on anyone. Talking about it and resting lets the feeling settle, and your coordinator may know of debriefs or other support. Hiding it or staying busy tends to keep the weight. If the feeling does not ease, a counsellor or doctor can help.
  - question: You have too many tasks and not enough time before Friday. What is the most useful step?
    options:
      - text: Say yes to everything and work faster.
      - text: List what is on your plate, tell your coordinator and ask which tasks can wait.
        correct: true
      - text: Do the easiest tasks and ignore the rest.
    explanation: Sharing the full list gives your coordinator the information to decide what matters most, and it takes away the pressure of carrying it alone. Working faster rarely fixes an overload, and quietly dropping tasks leaves surprises for others.

Read these situations and think about how you would handle the pressure.

At an online campaign, a post you published has received angry comments and you feel your face getting hot.

At a sports club, you are in charge of a tournament and the referee does not arrive thirty minutes before the first match.

At work, your manager asks you to take over a colleague's urgent project while you finish your own.
