---
title: What team work means
---

This is the GloRe definition:

> Ability to effectively contribute as a member of a team.

The sentence is short, and each part of it carries something to learn: you contribute, you do it effectively, and you do it as a member of a team.

## Contributing

Contributing begins with doing your own part. If you say you will bring the sound equipment on Friday, the team plans around that, and a missing speaker affects everyone's evening. Reliability is the base of team work, and it is easy to practise: keep your promises, or say early that you cannot.

Contributing also means offering what you know. In a group project at university, one person is good with data, another writes well and a third likes presenting. A team that sees these strengths and uses them produces better work than one where everybody does a bit of everything.

## Effectively

Effective team work needs communication. Listen when others speak, say what you think in a way people can hear, and check that everyone understands the plan. Quiet teammates often have useful ideas, and a good contributor makes space for them by asking, "What do you think?"

Effectiveness also depends on how you handle disagreement. At a youth camp, two volunteers want different games for the afternoon. The team talks through both, picks one and agrees to compare how it went. You may not get your first choice, and you can still help the chosen option work. A concern can go to the review afterwards, where it can improve the next camp.

## As a member of a team

A team shares a goal, and the goal comes before any single person's success. This changes small choices. You notice when a teammate is overloaded and you help. You ask for help yourself without feeling weak, because a problem shared early is smaller. You also share credit: when the coordinator thanks the group for a good event, you mention the people whose work was less visible.

At work, a customer support team has a rule that whoever finishes first takes a ticket from the longest queue. Nobody tracks who helped more, and response times fall for everyone.

Being a member also means respecting differences. People in a team have different ages, languages, speeds and habits. In an international volunteering group, one person may speak little in meetings and write long, careful messages afterwards. Knowing this, you can ask for input in both ways.
